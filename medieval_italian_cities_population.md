# How Medieval Italian Cities Were Populated — Key Studies & Numbers

*Scope: Centre-North Italy, c. 1000–1500. Figures checked against sources where possible; weaker sources are flagged ⚠️.*

---

## 0. The core mechanism

1. **High-pressure demographic regime.** Life expectancy at birth ≈ **20 years**, birth rate ≈ **50‰**, high adult mortality (Barbiera & Dalla-Zuanna 2024).
2. **Cities could not sustain themselves.** Urban mortality was higher than rural, so city growth depended on **rural → urban migration** (*inurbamento*).
3. **The plague reversed the flow.** After 1348 the countryside emptied, and cities had to offer incentives (oxen, favourable contracts) to get peasants to go *back* to the land (Cohn 2002, cited in Barbiera & Dalla-Zuanna).
4. **Growth came from within Italy.** Population swings were driven by plague, mortality and marriage age, **not** by migration into or out of Italy.

## 1. Macro picture — Italy vs Europe

**Sources:** Barbiera & Dalla-Zuanna 2024; Lo Cascio & Malanima 2005; Breschi & Malanima 2002; Malanima 1998/2005.

### Total population of Italy

| Period | Population |
|---|---|
| 5th c. AD (end of Antiquity) | ~11 M |
| 6th c. | ~8 M |
| Just before the Black Death (1347) | ~12.5 M |
| Late Middle Ages (post-plague) | ~7–8 M |

- Growth from the 9th century to 1347 was about **+50%**, mostly because plague was absent.
- Stagnation in the 6th–8th and 14th–16th centuries coincides with recurring plague.
- Marriage age also mattered: women married **younger** during the growth phase (9th c.–1347) and **later** during the stagnant phases.

### Density and urbanization c. 1300

| Region | Density (people/km²) | Share living in towns ≥5,000 |
|---|---|---|
| Italy (all) | 40 | — |
| Centre-North Italy | 48 | **21%** |
| Europe (excl. Russia) | 14 | 9% |
| England & Wales | 23 | 4% |

### Italy's weight in Europe's urban system (Malanima)

| Year | Italian share of Europe's urban population |
|---|---|
| 1300 | 30.5% |
| 1500 | 38.9% |
| 1800 | 15.9% |

- Italy's own urbanization rate fell from **21.4% to 16.2%** between the mid-14th and 19th centuries (Malanima 2005).
- Malanima's database covers every Italian centre with **≥5,000 inhabitants, 1300–1800**, measured about once per century. It is the standard dataset.
- He warns that the 5,000 threshold includes "agro-towns," meaning towns full of farmers. The 10,000 threshold is the cleaner definition of a "real city."

### Malanima's three phases

1. **10th c.–1350:** fast growth, often in *new* centres. Big 13th-century gainers include Alessandria, Cuneo, Ferrara, Udine, Venice, L'Aquila, Viterbo, Foggia and Lecce.
2. **1350–1861:** long relative decline.
3. **After Unification (1861):** modern growth.

Around 1000 the pattern was the reverse of later centuries: the South and the coasts led. Palermo had more than 100,000 inhabitants; the traditional estimate of 350,000 is too high.

## 2. Florence — the best-documented case

### Growth curve (Day 2002, *Journal of Medieval History*; Fiumi 1950/1958)

| Year | City population | Source / basis |
|---|---|---|
| c. 1175 | ≤10% of a contado of ≥100,000 | Day (conjectural) |
| 1199 | ≥ ~10,000 (minimum) | Oath lists |
| 1200 | 50,000 | Fiumi (revised) |
| 1260 | 75,000 | Fiumi |
| 1280 | 85,000 | Fiumi |
| 1300 | 95,000 | Fiumi |
| 1338 | 90,000 | Villani, via Fiumi |
| 1346–47 | possibly ~120,000 | 94,000 people fed by public bread distribution, taken as ≈ 4/5 of the city |
| 1347 | 76,000 | Fiumi |
| 1349 | 32,000 | Fiumi (immediately post-plague) |
| 1351 | ~50,000 | Standard secondary figure |
| 1427 | ~40,000 (Catasto); ~55,000 (Bosker et al. dataset estimate) | Catasto likely under-counts slightly |

- The contado (the city's rural territory) reached roughly **300,000** people.
- **Migration was the engine.** Day concludes that Florence's urban growth was fuelled *above all* by immigration from the contado.
- **Who the migrants were.** Plesner (1934) found that documented 13th-century immigrants were mostly rural **landowners**. Day argues this is a source bias: landless peasants and poor farmers were almost certainly the large majority but left fewer records.
- **Black Death, 1348** ⚠️ (popular source): about 60–80 deaths/day in April, ~100/day by mid-June, and ~400/day in July–August. Mortality for the year was ≥20× normal.
- **Repeat epidemics** (1340, 1363, 1374, 1400, 1417) kept the city near its low point for about 80 years.

### The 1427 Catasto (Herlihy & Klapisch-Zuber 1978/1985)

**What it covers:**
- About **60,000 households** and **260,000 people** across the Florentine state.
- **9,780 households** in the city itself, with about 20 variables each: age, occupation, number of mouths to feed (*bocche*), assets, debts.
- It is searchable online (Brown University Online Catasto).

**Marriage and household findings:**

| Indicator | Value |
|---|---|
| Age at first marriage, men | ~27 |
| Age at first marriage, women | ~18 |
| Never married by age 47–52 | 0.45% of men, 0.50% of women (marriage near-universal) |
| Share of women widowed at age 30 / 40 / 50 | 3% / 10% / 24% |

**Wealth findings:**
- **14.6%** of city households (1,431) had no taxable wealth.
- The top **137 households** held **29.8%** of the city's assessed wealth.
- The Catasto records 99 occupations and 1,210 family names.

**Town vs country:**
- Towns had more one-person households; the countryside had more married couples.
- Household size rose with wealth.
- The nuclear (conjugal) family dominated.
- These patterns resemble late-medieval England, where rural-urban migration was likewise central.

## 4. Piedmont — *villenove* and *borghi franchi*

**Sources:** Comba & Settia 1993; Panero 2004 & 2012; Comba, Longhi & Rao 2015.

These were a **second channel** of population movement. Communes and lords **founded new towns** and offered franchises (tax and legal privileges) to pull peasants out of scattered villages.

- **Timing:** foundations cluster between the **second half of the 12th century and the end of the 13th**.
- **Abandoned villages:** a survey of 182 sites identified about **20 villages** abandoned with reasonable certainty because their people moved into new or franchised centres, plus about **10 more** probable cases.
- **Examples of immigration *villenove*:** Moncalieri, Villastellone, Pecetto, Villanova d'Asti, Buttigliera d'Asti, Poirino. Frossasco, near Pinerolo, is documented as a *villa nova franca*.

### Town sizes (Sandri, in Ginatempo & Sandri 1990)

| Town | Estimate |
|---|---|
| **Vercelli** | 2,500–3,000 (12th–13th c.) → ~10,000 (late 1200s), per Panero. Reportedly lost **two-thirds** of its population in the 1361 epidemic. |
| **Chieri** | ~7,000 (13th-century land register) |
| **Pinerolo** | Series built from the 1351 land register (*catasto*) |

### How these numbers are made

Most figures come from **hearth (*fuoco*) counts × a multiplier**. Sandri uses **4–5 persons per hearth** as a min/max bracket. Tax registers also omit exempt people. Error bars of ±20–25% are therefore built in.

## 5. Quantitative/physics angle — urban scaling

**Source:** Cesaretti, Lobo, Bettencourt, Ortman & Smith 2016, *PLoS ONE*.

**Data:**
- **173 cities, c. 1280–1320**, including **30 in North/Central Italy**. The other 143 are in England, France/Belgium and Germany.
- Italian populations come from Malanima's data.
- Settled area was measured from maps and includes suburbs outside the walls. Population and area come from independent evidence.

**Model:** settled area scales with population as **A = a·Nᵅ**. Theory predicts the exponent **α between 2/3 and 5/6** if people mix freely across the city. If institutions (guilds, parishes, kin groups) segregated interaction, α would move toward **1** and larger cities would not get denser.

**Results:**

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Fitted α, by region and pooled | **0.71–0.79** |
| 95% confidence interval | excludes 1 in every case |
| Prefactor *a* (baseline area per person) | 0.05–0.12 |
| Differences between regions | not statistically significant |

**Interpretation:**
- **Larger medieval cities were denser**, since density scales as *n ∝ N^(1−α)*.
- The pattern resembles modern cities, though medieval α sits closer to 2/3 (modern values are nearer 5/6).
- There is no evidence that medieval institutions strongly restricted social mixing.
- **Italy-specific:** city walls constrained suburban sprawl more than in England or France. The authors expected this to make Italian cities denser, but the Italian exponent is statistically the same as elsewhere.
- **The dataset is open** (CSV in the supporting information), so you can reproduce the fit yourself.

## 6. Related econometric work (Italy)

- **Bosker, Brakman, Garretsen, de Jong & Schramm (2008)**, "Ports, plagues and politics," *European Review of Economic History* 12: 97–131.
  - Panel regressions of Italian city growth, 1300–1861.
  - Finds strong **North–South differences** in the factors driving growth century by century.
  - Earlier working-paper version: tests Zipf's law on the Italian city-size distribution and finds the dispersion of city sizes increasing over time.
- **Alfani (2019)**, *Economic History Review*: long-run effects of the 1629–30 plague on Italian cities. This is early modern, but it is a useful methodological template.
- **Barbiera & Dalla-Zuanna (2024)**, *Population and Development Review* 50(2): 541–570.
  - The best current synthesis of medieval Italian demography.
  - Combines cemetery skeletal data, land registers (including Legnago 1430, about 2,000 people) and the Catasto.

---

## 7. Caveats worth keeping in mind

- **Pre-1400 figures are reconstructions**, not counts. They are built from hearth lists, men-at-arms counts × multipliers (Herlihy used 3.5–4.0) and chroniclers such as Villani. Different multipliers shift results by ±25%.
- **Southern Italian urban data are weaker** than Centre-North data (Malanima says so explicitly).
- **Direct evidence on migrants is biased toward property holders**, because the poor left no records. Isotope work, as done for London, has barely been applied to Italian urban cemeteries yet; that is an open research gap.
